Taskonomy: Disentangling Task Transfer Learning
Authors: Amir Zamir, Alexander Sax, William Shen, Leonidas Guibas, Jitendra Malik, Silvio Savarese
Venue: CVPR 2018 best paper
First: 2018-04-23T10:46:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:19:33+00:00
Comments: CVPR 2018 (best paper). See project website and live demos at http://taskonomy.stanford.edu/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Do visual tasks have a relationship, or are they unrelated? For instance, could having surface normals simplify estimating the depth of an image? Intuition answers these questions positively, implying existence of a structure among visual tasks. Knowing this structure has notable values; it is the concept underlying transfer learning and provides a principled way for identifying redundancies across tasks, e.g., to seamlessly reuse supervision among related tasks or solve many tasks in one system without piling up the complexity. We proposes a fully computational approach for modeling the structure of space of visual tasks. This is done via finding (first and higher-order) transfer learning dependencies across a dictionary of twenty six 2D, 2.5D, 3D, and semantic tasks in a latent space. The product is a computational taxonomic map for task transfer learning. We study the consequences of this structure, e.g. nontrivial emerged relationships, and exploit them to reduce the demand for labeled data. For example, we show that the total number of labeled datapoints needed for solving a set of 10 tasks can be reduced by roughly 2/3 (compared to training independently) while keeping the performance nearly the same. We provide a set of tools for computing and probing this taxonomical structure including a solver that users can employ to devise efficient supervision policies for their use cases.

Decomposing Refusal Steering in Mixture-of-Experts Models
Authors: Anna C. Marbut, Daniel R. Olson, Travis J. Wheeler
First: 2026-06-02T19:23:46+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T16:57:45+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Safety alignment in instruction-tuned large language models (LLMs) depends on a model's ability to reliably refuse harmful or disallowed requests. Recent work has shown that a steering vector can be applied to a dense LLM during inference to suppress refusal behavior and induce responses to harmful requests. We extend this refusal steering method to three open-source Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLMs to decompose and better understand how refusal mechanisms operate across MoE components. We find that steering performance is uninhibited by the complex routing patterns inherent to the MoE architecture, and that a single expert recovers 78% of the full steering effect on average when free to select its own location. However, expert-level steering only recovers 54% on average when constrained to the location used by full-layer steering, a gap that widens further under safety-related system prompts. Our results also show that refusal signals captured by these steering methods differ from those captured by expert routing behavior. Together, these results suggest that refusal behavior is distributed unevenly across the MoE LLM architecture rather than governed by a single unified mechanism.

KITE: Decoupling Kinematics and Interaction for Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Manipulation
Authors: Qianxu Wang, Shengmiao Samuel Jin, Kuan Fang
First: 2026-06-20T15:51:30+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T16:14:34+00:00
Comments: 13 pages, including appendix. Project website: https://kite-manip.github.io/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Generalizing manipulation policies across robot embodiments remains difficult because standard policies tie the interactions needed for a task to the actions of a particular robot. We study zero-shot cross-embodiment manipulation, where a policy trained on source embodiments must be deployed on a structurally different target embodiment without additional task demonstrations. We introduce Kinematic Interaction Transfer across Embodiments (KITE), which decouples manipulation into embodiment-agnostic interaction prediction and embodiment-specific kinematic decoding, connected through a learned representation of interaction intent based on contact patterns. An interaction policy is trained to predict the intent using demonstration data only collected on a source robot, and shared across morphologically different target robots. A kinematic decoder realizes these intents by learning from synthetic data generated from each embodiment's kinematic model. Through such decoupling, KITE can robustly transfer learned skills to a new embodiment given only its kinematic model, without recollecting any task-specific demonstration data. We evaluate KITE on four manipulation tasks in simulation and the real world spanning transfer between parallel grippers, dexterous hands, and composite embodiments. KITE successfully achieves zero-shot transfer to structurally different target embodiments, and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines. Project website: https://kite-manip.github.io/.

The Instruction Tax on Factual Recall: Base Models Can Beat Their Instruction-Tuned Counterparts
Authors: Christophe Khalil, Aya Mourad
First: 2026-10-20T15:48:56+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T15:48:56+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We present our solution to the AKBC LM-KBC 2026 Shared Task, where we achieved a score of 0.6836 on the official leaderboard, more than double the baseline of 0.2964. This task requires generating all possible objects for subject-relation pairs using a language model with up to 32 billion parameters, with no retrieval or fine-tuning involved. We show that the prompting methodology is as important as the model architecture: while the instruction-tuned model version achieves 0.5681 with its chat interface, the base model achieves 0.6836 when prompted using text completion. Thus, instruction tuning decreases factual recall; we refer to this effect as the instruction tax, which is caused by the model's weights rather than the prompting method. Our final solution is fairly straightforward: we prompt the base model with some examples from the training data, generate 64--256 outputs, and retain only the repeated ones. Two specialized methods are then applied for each relation: one improves the recall of large answer sets, and the other verifies a person's vitality before predicting their place of death.

Confessions of a Masked MDP
Authors: Sebastian Reboul, Lorenzo Croissant, Hélène Halconruy
First: 2026-10-20T15:22:31+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T15:22:31+00:00
Comments: 18 pages, 7 figures Submitted to AISTATS 2027
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
In reinforcement learning, one presumes the action set to be known ahead of time, and, thus, one must know if any state-action pairs are not permissible ahead of computations. In some applications, determining this action mask is the main computational bottleneck. Isolating this problem to highlight its core properties, we study how to combine the search for an optimal policy with the learning of the mask itself, under a linear MDP model in which each attempted action reveals the exact value of a linear score determining its validity. In this framework, we first construct an algorithm based on deterministic optimistic optimization and show that it is minimax optimal, which we show is also computationally efficient. However, it is sub-optimal on non-degenerate instances, as we show. We propose an alternative, which is also computationally efficient, which plans its exploration strategy. This algorithm admits a formal interpretation as an analogue of information-directed-sampling for deterministic feedback, which may be of independent interest. Finally, we study an application of this framework to transfer learning, where the invalid action set changes over time.

Temporal Evidence-Parameter Co-Alignment for LLM Reasoning over Temporal Knowledge Graphs
Authors: Jiapu Wang
First: 2026-10-20T13:16:17+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T13:16:17+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) often struggle with temporally grounded reasoning because their static parametric memory is misaligned with the dynamic evolution of real-world knowledge. Although RAG and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) provide practical ways to enhance LLMs, they remain insufficient for temporal reasoning. Existing retrieval methods typically prioritize semantic relevance while overlooking temporal validity, event order, and evolutionary dependencies, resulting in evidence that is relevant in isolation but temporally fragmented. Meanwhile, conventional PEFT methods usually employ fixed and input-agnostic low-rank updates, making it difficult to capture time-dependent entity semantics and relation drift. To address these limitations, we propose Temporal Evidence-Parameter Co-Alignment (TEPCA), a framework for LLM reasoning over TKGs. TEPCA aligns LLM reasoning with temporal knowledge at both the evidence and parameter levels. At the evidence level, it introduces time-constrained Dynamic Relevance-Guided Path (DRGP) pruning to preserve globally coherent temporal paths, together with Mutual-Information (MI)-based pruning to retain query-informative local facts under temporal constraints. At the parameter level, it integrates Dynamic-Gated LoRA (DG-LoRA), a timestamp-conditioned low-rank adaptation mechanism that dynamically modulates the effective adaptation subspace according to temporal context. This design enables LLMs to better capture fine-grained entity evolution and time-dependent relation semantics while preserving parameter efficiency. Experimental results on two TKG benchmarks demonstrate that TEPCA achieves state-of-the-art performance. Additional evaluations of DG-LoRA on eight commonsense QA datasets further verify its general input-adaptive capacity, scalability across LLM backbones, and interpretability.

Dynamic Cross-Modal Prompt Generation for Multimodal Continual Instruction Tuning

First: 2026-05-11T15:59:06+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T13:03:32+00:00
Comments: Accepted to NeurlPS 2026. Code is available at https://github.com/LAMDA-CL/NeurIPS2026-Drape

Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) achieve strong performance through instruction tuning, yet real-world deployment often requires continual capability expansion across sequential tasks. In such scenarios, Multimodal Continual Instruction Tuning (MCIT) aims to acquire new capabilities while limiting catastrophic forgetting. Existing methods mainly follow a module-composition paradigm: they maintain task-level prompts or LoRA experts and dynamically route or aggregate a subset of them at inference. However, samples within the same task can still differ substantially in visual scenes, question intents, and reasoning demands. This motivates instance-level adaptation to individual query-image pairs rather than only selecting or combining task-level modules. To this end, we propose DRAPE (Dynamic Cross-Modal Prompt Generation), a prompt-learning framework that synthesizes continuous instance-specific soft prompts for MCIT. Instead of selecting prompts from a fixed pool, DRAPE derives prompt queries from the textual instruction and cross-attends to visual patch features, producing prompts conditioned on query-image pairs that are prepended to the frozen LLM. To mitigate forgetting during sequential updates, DRAPE applies null-space gradient projection to the shared projector and uses CLIP-based prototype routing for task-label-free generator selection at inference. Extensive experiments on MCIT benchmarks show that DRAPE achieves state-of-the-art performance among representative prompt-based and LoRA-based continual-learning baselines. MuPACE: Learning to Pace Muon for LoRA Fine-Tuning
Authors: Jan Kobiolka, Christian Frey, Josif Grabocka
First: 2026-10-20T11:52:36+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T11:52:36+00:00
Comments: Under Review
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Learning rate schedules are typically fixed before training begins, yet when fine-tuning large language models with low-rank adapters (LoRA), the appropriate scale varies substantially with the model, task, and adapter configuration. Practitioners re-tune a schedule for every new problem, yet a tuned schedule still cannot react to the training dynamics it encounters. We instead amortize learning rate selection across LoRA fine-tuning problems. We leave the base optimizer, Muon, untouched, so that its normalized update fixes the direction, and meta-learn only the global step size. Our method MuPACE is a small recurrent policy, meta-trained across a distribution of fine-tuning runs, that conditions on the observed trajectory and emits bounded adjustments to an interpretable analytic schedule. On 64 fine-tuning problems, MuPACE outperforms standard fixed schedules at a strong shared default on 64-78% of problems and is on par with the same schedules with per-task tuned peaks. Meta-trained only on models with up to 16 layers, MuPACE transfers one-shot to unseen tasks on models as deep as 64 layers, such as Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.8-27B, where a single run remains competitive with swept schedules.

Abstract
Understanding how supervision signals shape semantic visual representations remains challenging, particularly in fine-grained, low-data settings. Standard evaluations of vision models rely on downstream accuracy, which often fails to capture structural differences in learned representations. We propose a compact explainability-driven framework for comparing encoders trained with classification-only and caption-generation supervision on CUB-200. Using a controlled two-stage protocol in which both encoders are ultimately optimized for the same classification task, we enable a direct comparison of the resulting representations. Saliency maps are generated using attention rollout and gradient-weighted rollout and evaluated with five metrics covering faithfulness (positive and negative perturbation) and localization (keypoint emphasis, background attribution, and Earth Mover's Distance). Across random seeds and explanation methods, caption supervision yields representations that are more sensitive to the removal of salient regions, better aligned with annotated object parts, and less reliant on background regions, whereas classification supervision produces representations that are slightly more robust to the removal of low-saliency regions. An ablation study on the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset shows consistent trends across key metrics. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework makes supervision-induced representational differences observable and quantifiable beyond downstream accuracy alone. More broadly, it provides a systematic basis for assessing learned representations and comparing alternative training and transfer-learning strategies.

Poisoning-Based Backdoors Are Not Cryptographic
Authors: Masahiro Kaneko
Venue: NeurIPS 2026
First: 2026-10-20T11:27:30+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T11:27:30+00:00
Comments: NeurIPS 2026
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
To defend against backdoor attacks on neural networks, the defender must identify the verifier function that determines whether an input is a trigger, rather than rejecting individual triggers in isolation, since an attacker can generate new triggers satisfying the same verifier faster than they can be blocked. If a backdoor verifier could be installed with the asymmetry of a cryptographic authentication scheme, in which only the attacker can produce trigger inputs while the defender cannot reverse-engineer the verifier from observations, learning-based defense would be impossible in principle. We show that no such asymmetry can arise from poisoning-based installation, in which the only verifier-dependent input to training is a poisoned dataset. The argument is a reduction. A training procedure that installs a verifier from poisoned data, so that the backdoor fires on unseen triggers while benign behavior is preserved, is itself a learner for that verifier. Any verifier that is computationally hard to learn is therefore impossible to install through poisoning, and any verifier that poisoning can install is reconstructible by a white-box defender holding comparably many labeled trigger observations. A complementary argument shows that a secret key supplied to the model at inference is observable to the same defender. The result takes representative trigger observations as given and concerns when the backdoor fires rather than what it does. Experiments on instruction-tuned large language models confirm the dichotomy, with no configuration yielding a backdoor that is both effective for the attacker and irrecoverable for the defender.

Abstract
Dense linear probes assess task readability from language model (LM) representations, but do not directly reveal how many features suffice for prediction. We measure readout sparsity with \(k^*\), the smallest feature count found by a fixed search protocol that recovers 95% of dense-probe AUROC above chance. We evaluate 19 LMs from five labs on 14 core tasks. Ten base models give the nine reliably readable tasks similar feature counts and nearly the same order. This agreement extends to the selected features themselves, which on three tasks show cross-model response correspondence beyond class-mean differences and a label-correlation-matched null. Instruction-tuned deployment selectively reduces \(k^*\), with both weights and input format contributing. In controlled synthetic experiments across 20 models, successfully learned targets admit single-feature readouts, whereas readable controls require tens of features. Reference-model \(k^*\) is associated with label requirements across eight students, following an empirical scaling of \(N^*\propto(k^*)^{0.65}\). After continual learning, saved heads can fail while linear readability remains high, and recovery requires fewer labels than initial task learning under the tested procedures. Pruning reduces linear readability, with wider pre-pruning readouts generally associated with larger losses. These findings distinguish task readability from readout sparsity and establish their complementary value for predicting learning requirements and diagnosing LM changes.

Abstract
Stable and efficient training of ReLU networks with large depth is highly sensitive to weight initialization. Improper initialization can cause permanent neuron inactivation dying ReLU and exacerbate gradient instability as network depth increases. Methods such as He, Xavier, and orthogonal initialization preserve variance or promote approximate isometry. However, they do not necessarily regulate the pre-activation mean or control activation sparsity, and their effectiveness often diminishes in very deep architectures. This work introduces a semi-orthogonal initialization specifically optimized for ReLU by solving an optimization problem on the Stiefel manifold, thereby preserving scale and calibrating the pre-activation statistics from the outset. A family of closed-form solutions and an efficient sampling scheme are derived. Theoretical analysis at initialization shows that the proposed initialization mitigates dying ReLU, slows the decay of activation variance, and alleviates gradient vanishing, thereby stabilizing signal and gradient flow in deep architectures. Empirically, across MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, multiple tabular datasets, few-shot settings, and ReLU-family activations, the proposed initialization maintains stable performance as network depth increases, with particularly clear advantages in few-shot learning and large-depth settings.

Abstract
Transfer learning, in regression or classification, concerns problems where one aims to use imperfect data from a source distribution P to improve prediction w.r.t. a target distribution Q. While there is now much theoretical understanding of the unsupervised setting of this problem (where no labeled target data is available), the supervised setting with labeled target data remains under-studied. We identify and characterize an interesting dichotomy in the rates achievable in the supervised setting: there is a weak transfer regime where optimal rates are determined by information in either the source or target dataset but not both, and a strong transfer regime where much faster target rates are achievable, accounting for complementary information in source and target data. Furthermore, while previous theoretical tools for the unsupervised setting can be extended to capture the weak transfer regime, we argue that they cannot capture the strong regime due to limitations inherent in the various notions of relatedness they rely on. Instead, we provide an appropriate refinement of relatedness notions---termed weak and strong moduli of transfer---that reveal the two regimes and lead to a nontrivial characterization of which pairs of distributions P and Q adhere to which regime. Finally, the above dichotomy reveals a new categorization of existing algorithmic approaches in terms of their optimality or lack-thereof across supervised regimes, and we provide a generic approach that remains nearly optimal in all regimes without prior distributional knowledge.

Beyond a Single Direction: Chain-of-Thought Disrupts Simple Steering of Refusal
Authors: Kia-Jüng Yang, Dominik Meier, Jiachen Zhao, Terry Ruas, Bela Gipp

Abstract
Large reasoning models (LRMs) generate chain-of-thought (CoT) traces before producing final outputs, introducing a dynamic internal state that may complicate control mechanisms such as refusal. Unlike instruction-tuned LLMs, where refusal is mediated by a single directional subspace, refusal in large reasoning models (LRMs) additionally depends on the CoT. In DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA-8B, activation steering reverses refusal in only 39% of cases when the CoT is kept fixed, but removing the CoT entirely increases this to 70%, indicating that the CoT actively reinforces refusal. In a two-stage intervention where the model regenerates its CoT under activation steering, refusal is reversed in 94% of cases, while the resulting CoT alone retains 48% of this effect even after steering is removed. This suggests that the CoT can carry and reconstruct the compliance signal independently. These findings indicate that refusal in LRMs is jointly encoded in residual stream activations and CoT. This joint activation makes LRM more robust against activation-level interventions alone, but exposes CoT to a possible alternative surface attack.

Abstract
Learning latent dynamics is central to assessing the current states and forecasting the future trajectories of high-dimensional time series. For locally-stationary latent dynamics $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z}_{<t}; \mathbf{c})$ with latent dynamics state $\mathbf{z}_t$ and environment variable $\mathbf{c}$, prior identifiability results have largely focused on $\mathbf{z}_t$ when conditioned on predefined label $u$ of dynamics environments. This leaves two limitations: reliance on predefined labels that hinder generalization to unseen environments, and a limited understanding of the identifiability of $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ which -- although offering important structural properties for the identifiability of $\mathbf{z}_t$ -- are learned jointly with $\mathbf{z}_t$.
