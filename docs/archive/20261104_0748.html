TrafficInstruct: A Multi-Task Network Traffic Dataset and Benchmark for Instruction Tuning
Authors: Zhengdao Li, Hui Wen, Zixuan Wan, Weifeng Zhang, Yan Wu, Benlong Wu, Nenghai Yu, Weiming Zhang, Kejiang Chen
First: 2026-11-02T15:44:53+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T15:44:53+00:00
Comments: 25 pages, including appendices. Dataset information and access instructions: https://github.com/zhengdao-li/TrafficInstruct
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Instruction tuning for multi-task network traffic analysis requires data with clearly defined sources, supervision targets, and evaluation conditions. We introduce TrafficInstruct, a newly collected dataset comprising raw packet captures and 55,000 structured supervised fine-tuning records across ten tasks: malware traffic detection, botnet detection, malicious DNS-over-HTTPS detection, Web attack detection, advanced persistent threat detection, encrypted VPN detection, Tor behavior detection, encrypted application/service classification, website fingerprinting, and application classification under concept drift. An automated workflow captures traffic from controlled software executions, attack experiments, and application activities. Labels are supported by task-specific collection evidence, including process or socket attribution, activity records, and execution outcomes. Filtering, sanitization, and task-specific transformations produce instructions, traffic observations, and structured answers. We verify sample sources and partition the dataset by complete collection sessions, keeping all samples from the same session within one partition. The benchmark defines allowed inputs, deterministic label mappings, fixed training/validation/test partitions, and task-wise scoring rules. We evaluate majority-class, random-forest, and ET-BERT baselines, an unadapted 27B language model, and independently trained task-specific LoRA adapters on a shared frozen base. Adapters are selected using validation data and evaluated on the corresponding test sets. Task-specific adaptation improves classification performance, supporting instruction tuning across diverse traffic-analysis tasks.

Specificity Bias and the Wikipedia Paradox: An xAI Analysis of AI Text Detectors under Domain Shift
Authors: Nico Morloher, Johannes Reschke, Mohamed Mady
First: 2026-11-02T15:22:34+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T15:22:34+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The widespread adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) demands reliable detection of human-authored versus synthetic text. However, current Transformer-based detectors often experience severe performance degradation during domain shifts and operate as opaque classifiers. This paper investigates the robustness and interpretability of fine-tuned Transformer models (DistilBERT, RoBERTa) by evaluating them against a custom out-of-distribution (OOD) dataset of hyper-local news synthesized via open-source LLMs (Llama 3, Mistral, Gemma). To analyze model decision boundaries, we apply a tripartite Explainable AI (xAI) framework using SHAP. Our analysis reveals three key phenomena: First, detectors often rely on a "Call-to-Action" syndrome inherent to instruction-tuned LLMs rather than generic machine text. Second, we observe a "Specificity Bias," where LLMs successfully bypass detection by hallucinating specific named entities and mimicking conversational imperfection. Finally, we illustrate the "Wikipedia Paradox," wherein formal, objective human writing is frequently misclassified as AI due to its low statistical burstiness. These findings demonstrate that purely statistical detection paradigms face systematic limitations in highly structured human domains, emphasizing the critical need for semantically-aware, interpretable detection architectures.

Bolek: A Multimodal Language Model for Molecular Reasoning
Authors: Frederic Grabowski, Jacek Szczerbiński, Maciej Jaśkowski, Kalina Jasińska-Kobus, Paweł Dąbrowski-Tumański, Tomasz Jetka, Bartosz Topolski
First: 2026-05-04T15:46:39+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T14:57:58+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Molecular property models increasingly support high-stakes drug-discovery decisions, but their outputs are often difficult to audit: classical predictors return scores without rationale, while language models can produce fluent explanations weakly grounded in the input molecule. We introduce Bolek, a compact multimodal language model that grounds natural-language reasoning in molecular structure by injecting a Morgan fingerprint embedding into an instruction-tuned text decoder. Bolek is fine-tuned on molecular alignment tasks, including molecule description, RDKit descriptor prediction, and substructure detection, and on downstream reasoning over 15 TDC binary classification tasks using synthetic chains-of-thought anchored in concrete molecular features. Across these tasks, Bolek outperforms its Qwen3-4B-Instruct base on all endpoints in yes/no mode and on 13 of 15 in chain-of-thought mode, raising mean ROC/PR AUC from 0.55 to 0.76. It also outperforms TxGemma-9B-Chat on 13 of 15 binary classification tasks despite being less than half its size. Bolek's explanations are more grounded than those of the baseline LLMs: it cites numerical descriptors 10-100x more often per chain-of-thought, and the cited values agree strongly with RDKit for key descriptors such as TPSA, MolLogP, and MolWt (Spearman rho = 0.87-0.91). Generalisation extends beyond the training panel: on 15 unseen TDC classification endpoints, Bolek matches TxGemma on five, and it produces non-trivial rank correlations on three held-out regression endpoints despite never seeing downstream regression during training. These results suggest that targeted modality injection and reasoning supervision tied to verifiable molecular features can yield compact, auditable molecular reasoning models.

Written Once, Read Differently How Language Models Own Answers Steer Their Next Ones
Authors: Marcus Armstrong
First: 2026-11-02T14:25:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T14:25:14+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
When a language model answers a yes/no question, that answer changes how it answers a different question about the same entity. We study this \emph{self-commitment} in 22 open-weight models from four families (0.5B--72B parameters), fixing the model's first answer and measuring the shift in its second across 3,600 question pairs about 120 entities. Each answer writes a \emph{commitment direction} into the residual stream. The similarity of two questions' directions predicts their coupling on held-out entities ($ρ= 0.43$--$0.85$), and ablating a single direction shared across topics leaves at most 17\% of the effect in every model. How this direction is read is far less stable: the reading circuit spreads across more attention heads as models grow, instruction tuning makes more questions read it in the opposite sense, and in one model changing only the system prompt reverses the circuit's sign. Ablating the direction while sparing the attention sink removes most of the coupling at under 1.3\% perplexity cost in 18 of 19 instruction-tuned models, though it shifts standalone answers in two. Without the sink spared, the direction's alignment with the leading principal component of ordinary text predicts which models pay a high cost. Written once, the commitment is read differently across scale, training and prompt.

When a language model answers a yes/no question, that answer changes how it answers a different question about the same entity.

Abstract
With the popularization of livestreaming applications, automatic camera tuning has become indispensable for ordinary users to enjoy high Quality of Experience (QoE). However, the existing Image Quality Assessment (IQA) methods face two challenges in camera tuning. First, they struggle to identify subtle quality difference between images, which is common in camera tuning. Second, the ability to explain fine-grained differences and to provide suggestions for camera tuning are even more lacking. To address these challenges, we first construct FGLive-Comments, an instruction tuning dataset with rich annotations of both preferences for fine-grained image pairs and quality reasoning from global and local visual evidence, which supports selective adjustment recommendations of camera parameters. Based on FGLive-Comments, we develop Q-CamTuning, a multimodal large language model for livestreaming camera tuning. Its progressive perception-evidence-guidance framework learns quality-aware visual representations, enhances fine-grained reasoning with global-local evidence, and maps quality issues to camera-parameter adjustment directions. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that Q-CamTuning outperforms existing IQA models in both quality scoring and fine-grained image pair ranking. User study also validates its ability to recommend camera tuning suggestions.

CAPT: Class-Aware Prompt Tuning for Federated Long-Tailed Learning with Vision-Language Model

First: 2025-03-10T07:17:15+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T12:07:25+00:00

Abstract
Effectively addressing the joint effects of non-IID data and long-tailed distribution remains a key challenge in federated learning. While fine-tuning vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP has shown promise in tackling non-IID scenarios, it often degrades tail-class performance when both heterogeneity and long-tailed distributions are present, even leading to overall performance falling below the zero-shot baseline. To overcome this, we propose Class-Aware Prompt Tuning (CAPT), a novel framework leveraging pre-trained VLMs to effectively learn from non-IID and long-tailed data. Specifically, CAPT introduces a dual-prompt mechanism that synergizes class-aware and general prompts, enabling the model to preserve class-specific information while capturing global trends. Furthermore, to guide the prompts in learning targeted knowledge, we propose a heterogeneity-aware clustering strategy with two key components: similarity-based clusters aggregate class-aware prompts to reinforce tail-specific knowledge, while heterogeneity-based clusters aggregate general prompts to balance diverse representations and promote robust generalization. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CAPT significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, e.g., by improving overall accuracy by 6.1% on ImageNet-LT and 8.8% on Places-LT over baselines, while also substantially enhancing tail-class performance across other benchmarks.

Sledgehammer or Scalpel? A Fine-grained Adaptive Framework for Implicit Hate Speech Detection
Authors: Han Wang, Hanqing Tao, Xuesong Wang, Yi Zhu
First: 2026-07-10T02:41:55+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T07:45:19+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Unlike explicit attacks with obvious profanity, implicit hate speech hides malice within seemingly compliant expressions through metaphors and contextual hints, making its detection in online content review challenging. Many PLM- or LLM-based methods apply the same processing pipeline across samples, which may overlook differences in information requirements and introduce unnecessary computation. We observe that online hate speech is not monolithic but manifests in varied forms. We therefore define three fine-grained categories: Shallow, Targeted, and Context-Dependent. Accordingly, we propose Fine-grained Adaptive Implicit Hate speech Detection (FAID), a novel framework that first performs fine-grained classification and then adapts to specific categories. Specifically, for Shallow samples with surface-identifiable intents, the framework adopts lightweight Prompt-Tuning for rapid classification; for Targeted comments that bind malicious intent to concealed targets, we design knowledge augmentation to iteratively refine the model and reveal hidden targets; for Context-Dependent comments lacking background information, we utilize an agentic framework that automatically generates prompts to evolve context, infer missing background information and identify ambiguous malicious intents. This adaptive architecture focuses computational resources on complex implicit samples while avoiding redundant reasoning for shallow samples. Experiments on four benchmark datasets demonstrate that FAID significantly outperforms SOTA baselines.

Abstract
Chain-of-thought (CoT) explanations can sound plausible while having little influence on a model's final answer. We study four rewards that use the policy's own signals to encourage reliance on generated reasoning: gradient sensitivity, confidence gaps, progressive confidence gains, and reasoning--prompt swaps. We add each reward to group relative policy optimization (GRPO) and evaluate two small instruction-tuned models on GSM8K and StrategyQA using reasoning edits, paraphrases, and prompt hints. Several configurations increase answer reliance to reasoning edits, while effects on accuracy and paraphrase robustness vary. We also observe answer copying at a high reward weight and high reliance in decomposition settings with poor task accuracy. The study compares practical objectives for reasoning reliance, confirms the trade-off between reliance and performance, and proposes four lightweight rewards that can improve reasoning reliance.

Abstract
Imitation Learning aims to learn skills from extensive observations and demonstrations for robots, so it suffers from data scarcity and environment generalization. The existing methods predominantly focus on imitation from in-domain tasks and consequently struggle with generalization to unseen tasks. To bridge this generalization gap, we propose the \textbf{D}ynamics-\textbf{A}ware \textbf{M}eta-\textbf{I}mitation (DAMI) framework. PixelBench: Can LLMs Understand and Generate Images Pixel by Pixel?
Authors: Haoquan Zhang, Feiyang Liu, Juanxi Tian, Wanhua Li, Haodong Duan, Weiyang Liu
First: 2026-11-02T05:10:12+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-02T05:10:12+00:00

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can generate visual assets through text-based formats such as SVG and CAD, but these symbolic representations describe images using high-level primitives rather than explicit raster values. We ask whether LLMs can instead generate pixel images directly through their existing vocabulary, without a learned image tokenizer or decoder. We introduce PixelBench, a benchmark for text-to-pixel (T2P) and image-to-pixel (I2P) generation across multiple resolutions, together with a complementary pixel-understanding evaluation. Models generate an RGB color palette followed by a grid of indices specifying the palette color of each output pixel. In T2P, the strongest models produce recognizable images directly from text. In I2P, the strongest models outperform palette-matched downsampling and specialized pixelization algorithms at low resolutions. Pixel-understanding performance correlates strongly with generation performance. A resolution-scaling study shows that pixel-generation performance often improves initially before declining at high resolutions. Finally, we introduce Pixel Reconstruction Tuning (PRT), which augments visual instruction tuning with caption-palette-grid trajectories. We post-train Molmo2-4B from its pretraining checkpoint by mixing general instruction data with our curated PRT data, improving performance across 16 visual-centric benchmarks, indicating that explicit pixel reconstruction can provide useful auxiliary supervision.

Abstract
We introduce a transfer learning framework for regression that leverages heterogeneous source domains to improve predictive performance in a data-scarce target domain. Our approach fits a conditional generative model to each source domain and calibrates a shared linear combination of the generated responses via conditional quantile matching at the observed target covariates. The calibration aligns the conditional response distributions while accommodating simultaneous shifts in both the covariate and conditional response distributions. The calibrated responses then serve as pseudo-labels to augment the target data for a broad class of downstream learning tasks. Theoretically, we derive an excess risk bound for an empirical risk minimizer (ERM) trained on the augmented data, separating the benefit of an enlarged training sample from errors due to density ratio estimation, source generator estimation, conditional calibration, and irreducible transfer bias. Empirically, simulations and real-data applications show that the proposed method can improve target-domain prediction when the transfer bias is small.

MetaPOCS: Meta-Learned Multi-Exit Unfolding for Budget-Aware Seismic Data Interpolation
Authors: Baisong Jiang, Chunxia Zhang, Xiaoli Wei, Anxiang Di, Nanqi Zhao, Deng Xiong

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Seismic data interpolation must reconcile reconstruction fidelity with the computational budgets of acquisition quality control and subsequent imaging. Fixed-depth unfolding networks are optimized for a single operating point, whereas their intermediate estimates may be insufficiently trained for early deployment. We present MetaPOCS, a meta-learned multi-exit unfolding framework for budget-aware seismic interpolation. Building on a spatial--spectral POCS reconstruction backbone, MetaPOCS supervises intermediate stages and preserves the acquired traces at every exit. A Meta-GF-based optimization procedure constructs exit-oriented parameter displacements from a common initialization and learns their fusion weights through an equal-exit outer objective. The weights vary across parameter tensors and respect the nested support of the reconstruction stages. We derive the resulting fusion hypergradient to characterize how the outer objective reallocates competing updates, and distinguish this mechanism from scalar loss reweighting. At inference, one checkpoint provides a set of reconstruction budgets through a latency-calibrated exit policy. The evaluation framework couples matched-cost interpolation comparisons with finite-update diagnostics and a fixed reverse-time-migration operator, connecting multi-exit optimization to waveform and imaging fidelity. MetaPOCS thus provides a unified formulation for studying the reconstruction--computation trade-off of seismic unfolding across deployment budgets.

Comments: 20 pages, 4 figures. Code, data and pre-registration: https://github.com/saad-aamir/sycophancy-direction
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Sycophancy in language models is well documented as a behaviour but poorly localised as a mechanism. We ask whether capitulation under user pushback, the abandonment of a correct answer when a user objects, corresponds to a linear direction in the residual stream, and whether such a direction emerges with scale. Across eight instruction-tuned models spanning 0.5B to 14B parameters in two families, under a pre-registered protocol with leave-one-question-out cross-validation, within-question permutation nulls, and a positive control, we find no usable direction below 7B and a direction that clears our validation gate at 7B, 8B and 14B. Ablating that direction in Qwen2.5-7B reduces the rate at which the model reverses a correct answer by 4.8 percentage points, roughly a quarter in relative terms, while the identical intervention on a gate-failing direction and on a marginally passing one produces no effect. Probe quality, not statistical significance, predicts causal relevance. Behaviourally, pushback destroys three to five times more correct answers than it repairs at 1B and reaches parity by 7B, falsifying our own pre-registered prediction, and a sign-reversing crossover between pushback types survives an order of magnitude of scale in Qwen and holds at 8B in Llama, with marginalisation over five paraphrases per type. We additionally quantify five measurement hazards, including a numerical-precision effect that changes roughly one episode label in ten.
Summary / 总结
Sycophancy in language models is well documented as a behaviour but poorly localised as a mechanism.

Authors: Adam Y. Ahmed, Abdullah G. Elafifi

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
A vote over a model's samples can only help when the samples disagree. For open-ended generation the binding question is therefore not how to combine samples but whether a model produces samples worth combining, a question test-time scaling has answered for math but not for free-form text. We study it at small scale with semantic cluster voting, a judge-free aggregator that groups a small instruct model's samples by meaning and returns the largest cluster's representative, with no reward model or self-judging. On a 250-prompt benchmark with three tiers of answer checkability, the same pattern holds across three model families (Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.2-3B, Gemma-2-2B) and two generation seeds: voting raises accuracy on the math control, where samples vary but stay checkable (a gain of +10 to +29 points across the three models), yet adds at most +0.30 judge points on open-ended text. On a larger 200-problem MATH-500 run with the primary model, the math gain is +8.5 points (95% CI [+4.0, +13.0]). Almost every open-ended sample falls into one meaning cluster, so there is little to select over. The collapse is consistent with instruction-tuning rather than model size, and raising decoding temperature does not recover usable diversity, because it costs more quality than it adds (net -0.64 judge points, 95% CI [-0.93, -0.37]).

Abstract
Automated classification of retinal pathologies from Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) images remains challenging because diagnostically relevant morphological patterns may exhibit substantial inter-class similarity. Although high classification accuracies have been reported on benchmark OCT datasets, cross-study comparison is limited by heterogeneous evaluation protocols and by the limited characterization of compact convolutional neural network (CNN) baselines trained from random initialization. This study addresses this gap through two contributions. First, it provides a focused review of CNN-based retinal OCT classification and identifies methodological considerations relevant to controlled benchmarking. Second, it presents ED126-A, a purpose-built four-class CNN baseline trained from scratch under a documented experimental protocol on the Kermany labeled OCT dataset. The model contains 1,241,380 total parameters, including 1,239,460 trainable parameters, and is evaluated using training-dynamics analysis, class-wise performance metrics, confusion-matrix analysis, confidence analysis, t-SNE representation-space visualization, Grad-CAM, hierarchical feature-map analysis, and one-vs-rest ROC/Precision-Recall analysis. On the image-level held-out test set, ED126-A achieved 95.03% accuracy, with a macro-F1 of 0.9205 and a weighted-F1 of 0.9521. The principal class-conditional limitation occurred for Drusen, which attained a recall of 0.9605 but a precision of 0.7493 and an F1-score of 0.8419. The confusion matrix identified Normal to Drusen and CNV to Drusen as major error channels. These results support the use of ED126-A as a documented from-scratch reference baseline and provide an empirical basis for controlled evaluation of transfer learning, increased depth, attention, multi-scale feature fusion, and calibration in subsequent studies.

Abstract
Effective multi-agent collaboration requires finalizing solutions from reasoning that is distributed across the individual attempts of many agents. However, useful intermediate reasoning can remain unexploited when collaboration is limited to voting on conclusions reached among individual agents, or aggregating long, noisy collections of reasoning trajectories in context. In this work, we propose TTT-Consolidate, a framework that uses multi-agent reasoning trajectories as adaptation data to improve a model's subsequent reasoning. TTT-Consolidate jointly consolidates trajectories into parametric representations through test-time training, then generates a new solution by conditioning on the consolidated information. We meta-learn how to consolidate using bi-level optimization at training time, where an inner loop encodes reasoning trajectories into a lightweight LoRA adapter, and an outer loop learns meta-parameters for the inner loop such that the consolidation is helpful for the model to synthesize a final solution. As no large-scale corpus of diverse reasoning trajectories exists for training aggregation methods, we construct the OpenParallelThinking corpus composed of 12,810 math problems paired with a mixture of trajectories from six LLMs. Evaluated on challenging math and STEM benchmarks, TTT-Consolidate outperforms the best aggregation baselines by up to 20 percentage points, even under matched compute and runtime settings, with the gap widening at larger model scales. TTT-Consolidate also recovers from unsuccessful individual trajectories more robustly, an ability that improves as the number of available (even incorrect) trajectories increases. Finally, we demonstrate that TTT-Consolidate benefits from both SFT and RL-based outer loop objectives. We will release TTT-Consolidate and OpenParallelThinking for use in future work on reasoning aggregation.

Abstract
Digital learning environments produce rich clickstream data that can reveal how students approach learning tasks, yet interpreting these fine-grained traces often requires substantial expert effort. We introduce ClickSight, a framework for interpreting student clickstreams through learning strategies defined in the literature. Given a student clickstream and a predefined set of learning strategies, ClickSight uses a staged LLM-based pipeline to identify strategy-relevant evidence, assess adherence to each strategy, and generate an evidence-grounded interpretation of the student's behavior. We evaluate ClickSight across two learning environments, 365 students, and 12 model backbones using an expert-grounded quality rubric and an LLM judge calibrated with human-expert ratings. Abstract
