TimeSeek: Multi-Task and Multi-Domain Pretraining for Generalized Time Series Analytics
Authors: Peng Chen, Yihang Wang, Zhongwen Rao, Yang Shu, Yuying Qiu, Yuxuan Chen, Lujia Pan, Bin Yang, Chenjuan Guo
First: 2026-11-10T18:49:26+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-10T18:49:26+00:00
Comments: Accepted by IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and MachineIntelligence
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Time series analytics has been widely applied across various domains. While existing time series foundation models (TSFMs) have demonstrated promising results, they still face two major challenges: 1) Most TSFMs mainly rely on stacking Transformer layers to increase parameter size, lacking the ability to adaptively model complex time series patterns from multi-domain data. 2) Current TSFMs are primarily pre-trained for specific tasks, e.g., forecasting or anomaly detection, and a few models support multiple tasks, but they fail to fully leverage task specificity and commonality, resulting in suboptimal performance. To address these challenges, we propose TimeSeek, a novel generalized time series analytics model that adaptively models time series patterns from multi-domain data and unifies multiple tasks in pre-training. Conceptually, TimeSeek is guided by selective information sharing under domain and task heterogeneity. From the perspective of multi-domain data, we propose a Multi-Domain Feature Extractor that adaptively captures temporal dependencies and channel correlations based on domain-specific characteristics, including progressive multi-scale modeling and adaptive slot channel modeling. From the perspective of the diversity of tasks, we propose multi-task collaborative training, which leverages task specificity and task commonality through task customization and cross-task training, effectively unifying multiple tasks in pre-training and better adapting to diverse downstream scenarios. After pre-training on large-scale time series data, TimeSeek achieves state-of-the-art performance in forecasting, anomaly detection, and classification tasks across both few-shot learning and zero-shot situations, together with favorable inference efficiency.

DnNP: Denoising Input Uncertainty in Neural Processes
Authors: Fatemeh Tohidian, Chengzhi Shi, Soomi Lee, Matthew Elia, Amy Mueller, Stratis Ioannidis, Jennifer Dy
Venue: NeurIPS 2026
First: 2026-11-10T18:00:37+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-10T18:00:37+00:00
Comments: Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. 20 pages, 3 figures, 3 tables. Code: https://github.com/iSUPER-ML/DnNP
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Neural Processes (NPs) are flexible meta-learning models capable of uncertainty quantification across diverse applications. However, the standard NP framework assumes access to noise-free inputs. This is unrealistic in many practical settings, where sensor noise or measurement errors are unavoidable. This discrepancy between model assumptions and real-world data can significantly degrade prediction quality and uncertainty estimates. We introduce Denoising Neural Processes (DnNPs), a principled extension of the NP framework that explicitly models input uncertainty. We train DnNPs via variational inference. DnNPs jointly learn to denoise corrupted inputs and perform meta-learning, making them robust to noisy training data. We demonstrate DnNPs' effectiveness on both synthetic and real-world datasets, showing consistent improvements in prediction and uncertainty quantification compared to standard NPs. On PM2.5 spatial interpolation from real-world air quality sensors, DnNP reduces RMSE by up to 30% and NLL by up to 73% relative to standard NPs. We also provide theoretical grounding: we show that the Bayes-optimal predictor under noisy inputs belongs to the DnNP model class, and that plug-in models with fixed observation variance incur an irreducible KL divergence gap that DnNP provably avoids.

Neural Processes (NPs) are flexible meta-learning models capable of uncertainty quantification across diverse applications.
A Margin Perspective on LoRA: Robustness to Catastrophic Forgetting and Adapter Merging (MaLoRA)
Authors: Ziqing Xu, Hancheng Min, Lachlan Ewen MacDonald, Salma Tarmoun, Enrique Mallada, Weijie Su, Rene Vidal
First: 2026-11-10T17:44:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-10T17:44:14+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is the de facto method for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large neural networks, achieving strong task adaptation with minimal trainable parameters and low optimization cost. Beyond this efficiency, practitioners have observed two striking properties: \textit{robustness to catastrophic forgetting} and the ability to \textit{merge independently trained adapters} into a single model that performs competitively across multiple tasks. These properties are central to continual and multi-task learning, yet remain poorly understood. In this work, we provide a theoretical explanation through a unified \textit{margin-based perspective}. We analyze LoRA in multiclass linear classification under a near-orthogonal task regime with $\ell_2$-regularization, and characterize the optimal LoRA adapter across regularization regimes. Our analysis shows that, in an intermediate regime of regularization parameters, the optimal adapter aligns with the \emph{max-margin solution} on the fine-tuning data. Building on this characterization, we derive two key consequences. First, we obtain closed-form expressions for the margins on pre-training and fine-tuning data, revealing a precise margin trade-off: the regularization parameter controls the balance between retention and adaptation. Second, we analyze adapter merging, proving that merged models achieve positive margin on each task and deriving optimal mixing coefficients that balance margins across tasks and maximize the margin over their union. These results lead to a simple, training-free merging rule, which we term \textit{margin-based LoRA merging} (MaLoRA). Experiments on modern architectures and real datasets validate our theoretical predictions, showing that MaLoRA matches or outperforms several adapter-merging baselines across a range of vision and language classification tasks.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly explored for resource management across the Cloud-Edge continuum. However, embedding LLMs directly inside latency-sensitive control loops places inference latency, execution jitter, and hallucination risks on the critical path. This study argues for a different use of LLMs: a continually instruction-tuned intent compiler that runs once per management intent or declared context change, above a fast, deterministic tactical controller that handles all low-latency runtime actions. We formulate intent compilation as a continual instruction tuning problem, because management intents arrive sequentially and accumulate while infrastructure and workload contexts drift. Unlike recent LLM-based management frameworks, our design scores the model's outputs by execution, so that catastrophic forgetting can be measured in the executed objective of the underlying controller. On a continuum simulator with a 2B backbone and parameter-efficient adapters, preliminary results show that naive sequential tuning forgets earlier intents in the executed objective, that replay of stored examples nearly prevents this forgetting, and that the tuned compact compiler performs on par with simpler translators while being more robust than a grammar parser to rephrased intents.

Conformal Risk Control for QoT-Aware Lightpath Feasibility Under Technology-Driven Domain Shift
Authors: Kiarash Rezaei, Omran Ayoub, Paolo Monti, Carlos Natalino
First: 2026-11-10T10:53:09+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-10T10:53:09+00:00
Comments: 8 pages; 4 figures; accepted for publication in the Journal of Lightwave Technology (JLT)
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML)-based regression models support operational decision-making in optical networks but typically expose only point predictions, which carry no statistically controlled information about their reliability. This paper proposes a post-hoc conformal risk control (CRC) framework that keeps a pretrained AI/ML regressor fixed, provides a finite-sample guarantee on uncertainty band miscoverage, and maps the band to operational decisions through policy rules. The guarantee applies to interval miscoverage, while decision-error rates are evaluated empirically. We instantiate the framework for quality of transmission (QoT)-aware lightpath (LP) feasibility assessment, in which a regressor predicts optical signal-to-noise ratio (OSNR) under domain shifts caused by fiber, amplifier, and switching upgrades. Feature shift contribution scores identify the LP features whose relation to QoT changes the most across a technology transition, and two policies translate the bands into feasibility decisions, allowing operators to tune the trade-off between conservatively rejecting or aggressively accepting LP realizations. Experiments on open space-division multiplexing (SDM) QoT datasets over the CONUS topology show that, for a target coverage of 90%, the uncertainty bands meet the target in all 170 trials spanning 17 domain shifts and ten calibration-test partitions, whereas a source-only quantile regression (QR) baseline meets it in none. Under severe shifts, the efficiency-oriented (EO) policy empirically improves zero-shot (ZS) feasibility-decision accuracy from about 85% to above 99%, approaching a transfer learning (TL) policy while avoiding retraining. The framework provides finite-sample control of uncertainty band miscoverage for regression-based optical network decision support, with potential applications beyond QoT-aware LP feasibility assessment.

Venue: NeurIPS 2026

Abstract
Watermarking schemes for large language model output are evaluated almost exclusively on English text using each scheme's detection threshold and a narrow set of quality measurements. Multilingual deployment exposes evaluation-design choices that are inconsequential on English but determine conclusions cross-lingually. We propose an evaluation framework with four components: detection thresholds calibrated empirically per deployment context, a threshold-independent companion measurement that distinguishes calibration failures from detection failures, three disjoint quality measurement paradigms (distributional, paired-semantic, and reference-perplexity), and a generalized-entropy decomposition of cross-language disparity over a typological family partition. Applied to six watermarking schemes, three open-weight generators, eleven languages spanning four scripts and eight typological families, and both base and instruction-tuned regimes, the framework reveals failure modes that single-language, single-paradigm evaluation cannot surface. Against a shape-matched null, detection disparity is predominantly between-family on the typological partition, indicating that detection gaps are structural to language properties rather than idiosyncratic to particular languages; quality disparity is large but not reliably family-structured.

Abstract
Large language models trained with reinforcement learning (RL) to reason step by step have been increasingly shown to outperform their supervised fine-tuned (SFT) counterparts on mathematical reasoning tasks. Yet the mechanistic basis for this improvement remains unclear. We therefore ask, what internal representational differences enable RL-trained reasoning language models to reason better? Our work presents two primary lines of evidence: First, layer-wise linear probes tend to predict answer correctness more accurately in RL-trained reasoning language models than in instruction-tuned models on synthetic math problems, although output length alone matches the DeepSeek-Math-RL probe on two templates. Second, mean ablation studies suggest that accuracy drop rises with layer depth in DeepSeek-Math-RL but not in DeepSeek-Math-Instruct. Finally, we analyse token-count variability across accuracy bins for RL-trained reasoning language models and instruction-tuned models to measure whether token counts vary more on harder problems. Token counts tend to vary more on lower-accuracy problems, although in DeepSeek-Math-Instruct only with truncated responses included. At high accuracy, we observe lower variability in DeepSeek-Math-RL than in DeepSeek-Math-Instruct, and we observe higher variability in Olmo-3-Think than in Olmo-3-Instruct.

Abstract
Historically automated electroencephalography (EEG) systems was done using task-specific supervised models trained on purposely annotated data. A more recent modeling paradigm relies on creating a unified representation, or a 'foundation model', for multiple modality-specific tasks through large-scale domain-specific pretraining on the unannotated EEG signal. The quality of this unified representation directly depends on the availability of the modality-specific datasets. At the same time, generalist Vision Language Models (VLMs) exhibit broad few-shot learning capacities as well as good fine-tuning adaptability to different visual and language tasks. Tracing Moral Foundations in Large Language Models
Authors: Chenxiao Yu, Bowen Yi, Farzan Karimi-Malekabadi, Suhaib Abdurahman, Jinyi Ye, Shrikanth Narayanan, Yue Zhao, Morteza Dehghani
First: 2026-01-09T00:09:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-09T23:17:45+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models often produce human-like moral judgments, but it is unclear whether this reflects an internal conceptual structure or superficial ``moral mimicry.'' Using Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) as an analytic framework, we study how moral foundations are encoded, organized, and expressed across 14 base and instruction-tuned LLMs spanning four model families (Llama, Qwen2.5, Qwen3-MoE, Mistral) and scales from 7B to 70B. We employ a multi-level approach combining (i) layer-wise analysis of MFT concept representations and their alignment with human moral perceptions, (ii) pretrained sparse autoencoders (SAEs) over the residual stream to identify sparse features that support moral concepts, and (iii) causal steering interventions using dense MFT vectors and sparse SAE features. We find that models represent and distinguish moral foundations in a manner that aligns with human judgments, and that this moral geometry naturally emerges from pretraining and is selectively rewired by post-training. At a finer scale, SAE features show clear semantic links to specific foundations, suggesting partially disentangled mechanisms within shared representations. Finally, steering along either dense vectors or sparse features produces predictable shifts in foundation-relevant behavior, demonstrating a causal connection between internal representations and moral outputs. Together, our results provide mechanistic evidence that moral concepts in LLMs are distributed, layered, and partly disentangled, suggesting that pluralistic moral structure can emerge as a latent pattern from the statistical regularities of language alone.

From Architecture to Output: Testing the Structural Origins of Hallucination and the Amplifying Role of Data
Authors: Md. Rejaul Korim Sadi, Toufiqur Rahman Tasin, Golam Mostofa Naeem
First: 2026-04-29T11:34:09+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-09T21:20:10+00:00

Abstract
Surveys of hallucination classify errors by how an output relates to its source, and often explain them by the model's structure: attention retrieves by association, the maximum-likelihood objective has no term for truth, and left-to-right decoding commits to early tokens. Data, on this account, makes these failures worse rather than causing them. The account is usually argued rather than tested. We test it with interventions that need only sampling access. Study 1 holds pretraining data, tokenizer and scale fixed and swaps attention for a state-space mixer, using five Pythia/Mamba pairs from 130M to 2.8B parameters and four further attention models, on 111 facts presented with a planted false or a planted true statement. Swapping the mixer leaves free-recall failure unchanged (both 2.8B models answer 3 of 200 SimpleQA questions), but it changes what happens to a defect in the input. From 410M upwards, every attention model repeats the planted falsehood more often than its matched state-space model (2.8B: 650 vs. 566 of 666 items, p = 2.5e-20), also on facts both models know, while the state-space models can still read planted facts they did not know. Study 2 replaces the first wrong fact in 111 erroneous answers from three instruction-tuned models. The correct fact cuts the share of answers with a downstream error from 92.5% to 48.1%, but a different wrong fact works as well (p = 0.38), and the models give the right answer in isolation on only 2 of 111 items. The structural account survives in a narrower form. The mixing component decides whether a defect in the context becomes an output error, commitment decides how an error spreads, and on long-tail questions the first error comes from missing knowledge, not from a suppressed truth.

Interpretability Is Not Model-Specific
Authors: Marcus Armstrong, Navid Ayoobi, Pradham Mummaleti, Alexander Chulzhanov, Arjun Mukherjee
First: 2026-11-09T16:43:26+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-09T16:43:26+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Interpretability tools are usually built for one model at a time: a refusal direction, a truthfulness probe, or a steering vector is found with labeled data inside the model it explains. We ask whether such tools can be carried to other models through a map between residual streams fit only on unlabeled prompts. We transfer a refusal direction, tested causally by addition and ablation, and two read-out tools, a correctness direction and a sentiment probe, across 110 pairs of models from five families and 1B to 72B parameters and between base and instruction-tuned models, with controls for specificity and five seeds. How much transfers depends on what the map was fit on. A map fit on ordinary questions recovers 54\% of the target's own refusal steering and 16\% to 77\% of its correctness gain; adding unlabeled prompts from the region where the tool operates raises these to 102\% and 88\% to 98\%, while control directions recover at most 1\%. We prove that a linear map behaves this way whenever two models correspond linearly on the data it covers, and a label-free coverage score derived from this result tracks recovery across more than 16,000 runs and explains earlier reports that maps between models fail across domains. Carried read-out tools outperform anything the target's unlabeled representations yield. Interpretability results, once found, can be reused across models at the cost of a few unlabeled prompts.

Authors: Anh-Tien Nguyen, Trung Dang, Nghiem Tuong Diep, Bui Ngoc Han Nguyen, Tan-Ha Mai, Miriam Cindy Maurer, Phuong Hoa Nguyen, Thi Thuy Uyen Nguyen, Youngjun Park, Daniel Sonntag, Duy Minh Ho Nguyen, Anne-Christin Hauschild

Abstract
Pathology vision-language foundation models vary in performance across diseases and tasks, with no single model consistently performing best. The high cost of expert pathology annotation can also limit the labeled data available for task-specific adaptation. Combining complementary pretrained representations is a potential approach to these limitations, yet learning an effective fusion from few labeled examples remains challenging. We introduce Few-shot Fusion Foundation Models of Computational Pathology (FFM-CP), which is a framework that combines multiple pathology vision-language models in the few-shot learning setting. The framework first aligns heterogeneous representations using a closed-form Orthogonal Procrustes transformation estimated from corresponding support images. This alignment preserves within-model feature geometry without training an additional alignment network. Within the aligned space, a unified graph enables information exchange across backbones by jointly refining support-image features and visual and textual class prototypes. These refined representations support complementary text-prototype and case-retrieval branches that capture semantic class knowledge and within-class visual variation, respectively. Each branch learns to combine predictions from all ordered backbone pairs, allowing queries encoded by one model to draw on evidence represented by another. We evaluate three backbone combinations on six histopathology datasets at 4, 8, and 16 shots per class. FFM-CP achieves higher mean macro-F1 than the strongest individually adapted member of each fused set in 50 of 54 comparisons. These findings suggest that combining complementary pretrained representations can improve histopathological classification when annotations are limited.

PRIM: Meta-Learned Bayesian Root Cause Analysis
Authors: Christopher Lohse, Anish Dhir, Amadou Ba, Bradley Eck, Marco Ruffini, Jonas Wahl
First: 2026-05-09T08:14:09+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-09T14:08:26+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Root Cause Analysis (RCA) aims to identify the main drivers of anomalies in complex multivariate systems. The task is challenging due to the need for structural causal knowledge for some methods, and the computational cost of inference at test time. This paper introduces PRIM (Prior-fitted Root cause Identification with Meta-learning), a causal meta-learning approach that frames RCA as a Bayesian inference task over a prior of causal models. By marginalising out structural uncertainty, PRIM detects changes in the data-generating mechanism between normal and anomalous periods, infers distributional differences without explicit statistical testing, and implicitly learns causal structure without model fitting at test time. Following the simulation-based meta-learning paradigm of prior-fitted networks (PFNs), PRIM uses a transformer neural process (TNP) that jointly attends over normal and anomalous samples and the structural dependencies of nodes, enabling fast zero-shot inference in systems with up to 100 variables. Across synthetic benchmarks and two realistic benchmark datasets, PetShop and CausRCA, PRIM is competitive with other graph-free methods.

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) aims to identify the main drivers of anomalies in complex multivariate systems.

Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) deliver strong performance across vision-language tasks but suffer from high autoregressive decoding latency. Although speculative decoding can accelerate Large Language Models (LLMs) without changing their output distribution, directly applying existing approaches to MLLMs yields limited speedups because text and visual tokens exhibit distinct characteristics. We present Multimodal Speculative Decoding (MSD), a framework specifically designed for efficient MLLM inference. MSD is motivated by two key observations: text and visual tokens should be handled separately during drafting, and an effective draft model requires both strong language modeling and visual perception capabilities. Accordingly, MSD decouples text and visual token processing in the draft model and applies token-specific strategies to improve drafting efficiency. We further introduce a two-stage training strategy. The draft model is first trained on text-only instruction-tuning data to strengthen its language modeling ability and is then progressively exposed to multimodal data to develop visual perception while preserving its language competence. Extensive experiments across multimodal benchmarks demonstrate that MSD achieves up to $2.29\times$ inference speedup for LLaVA-1.5-7B and $2.46\times$ for LLaVA-1.5-13B without sacrificing generation quality. These results establish MSD as an effective and general approach for accelerating multimodal autoregressive generation. Our code is available at https://github.com/Lyn-Lucy/MSD

Abstract
Direct pairwise AUC-surrogate training is attractive because it trains toward the ranking metric used at test time, especially under class imbalance. This creates a direct alignment between the training objective and the evaluation metric, but such metric alignment alone does not guarantee that stochastic training remains corrective. We study this gap through Corrective Power: whether pairwise mini-batch updates produce stronger and more reliable improvements for more severe positive-negative ranking errors. Our main observation is that direct pairwise training can enter regions where severe errors remain common but no longer produce useful corrective motion. In such regions, even the mini-batch noise that would normally help SGD move along the steepest severe-error correction direction can become much weaker. In this paper, we seek a theoretical explanation for this by exploring the degenerate property of the U-statistics formed from the pairwise gradients therein. Our results provide a mechanistic explanation for several empirical patterns: (a) squared-hinge loss is often an effective AUC surrogate; (b) cross-entropy warm-up can help models that enter the AUC phase from a poor starting region; and (c) parameter-efficient fine-tuning from a pretrained model can reduce that need by starting closer to a favorable ranking geometry. Experiments on 8 CV tasks and 8 NLP tasks, using ResNet50, DenseNet121, DistilBERT-base, and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, support these predictions.

Latent-space Attacks for Refusal Evasion in Language Models
Authors: Giorgio Piras, Raffaele Mura, Fabio Brau, Maura Pintor, Luca Oneto, Fabio Roli, Battista Biggio

Abstract
Safety-aligned language models are trained to refuse harmful requests, yet refusal behavior can be suppressed by steering their internal representations. Existing methods do so by ablating a refusal direction from model activations, aiming to remove refusal from the model's residual stream. Despite their empirical success, these methods lack a principled account of the latent-space transformation they induce and why it suppresses refusal. In this work, we recast refusal suppression as a latent-space evasion attack against linear probes trained to separate refused from answered prompts. Under this view, prior work's difference-in-means direction naturally defines such a probe, and its ablation is exactly a projection onto its decision boundary, i.e., a minimum-confidence evasion attack. This perspective not only explains the empirical success of prior work but also admits a key limitation: evasion stops at the decision boundary, motivating the need to push representations further into the compliant region, i.e., where the model answers. We leverage this by proposing a Controlled Latent-space Evasion attack that projects representations past the boundary with an optimized confidence. Across 15 instruction-tuned, multimodal, and reasoning language models, we achieve higher attack success rates than existing refusal-ablation baselines, also outperforming jailbreak attacks.

Abstract
Seismic phase picking is fundamental for microseismic monitoring and subsurface imaging. Manual processing is impractical for real-time applications and large sensor arrays, motivating the use of deep learning-based pickers trained on extensive earthquake catalogs. On a broader scale, these models are generally tuned to perform optimally in high signal-to-noise and long-duration networks and often fail to perform satisfactorily when applied to campaign-based microseismic datasets, which are characterized by low signal-to-noise ratios, sparse geometries, and limited labeled data. In this study, we present a microseismic adaptation of a network-wide earthquake phase picker, Phase Neural Operator (PhaseNO), using transfer learning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Starting from a model pre-trained on more than 57,000 three-component earthquake and noise records, we fine-tune it using only 200 labeled and noisy microseismic recordings from hydraulic fracturing settings. Abstract

Abstract
